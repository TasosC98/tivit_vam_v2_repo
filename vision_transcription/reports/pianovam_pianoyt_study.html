Where the work stands

We built a video-only piano transcription model that reads each of the 88 keys from its own fixed column of a flattened keyboard image — a per-key (“tiled”) model with 1.3 M parameters. We test it on two datasets that differ in how their labels were made: PianoVAM, whose labels come from a Disklavier piano, and PianoYT, YouTube videos whose labels were produced by an audio transcription model.

  • On PianoVAM it is on par with the state of the art (V2N, ISMIR 2026) at 50 ms and better at 100 ms, with 22× fewer parameters — same test files, same labels, same scoring script.
  • Replacing our 88 key columns by one global feature (ablation A1) costs 2.3 points at 100 ms and drops the model below V2N: the per-key design is what puts us ahead.
  • On PianoYT, pre-training on PianoVAM and fine-tuning reaches 69.9% onset F1 at 100 ms, on exactly the test set on which PPAN (IJCAI 2025) reports 68%.
  • Without fine-tuning the model collapses on the other dataset in both directions, as V2N also found for its models. Part of the collapse is a timing mismatch that we can measure and correct on the validation split.
  • PianoYT’s labels are out of sync in 14% of the videos (31 of 220, including 4 test videos); those 4 test videos alone cost every model about 2 points at 50 ms. Correcting the training labels did not help the fine-tuned model (E6ft); the from-scratch version (E6) is still training.

How our system works

The model is a visual version of Onsets and Frames, the classic audio transcription network. Instead of a spectrogram it reads an image of the keyboard. For every key and every video frame it answers two questions: “did a note start here?” (onset) and “is the key down?” (frame). No audio is used at any point.

  1. Video framePianoVAM: 1920×1080, 60 fps. PianoYT: mostly 1920×1080, 24–60 fps. We decode at 720p and sample 30 frames per second by timestamp.
  2. Flatten the keyboardThe dataset gives the keyboard corners (PianoVAM) or crop box (PianoYT). A perspective warp maps them to a straight strip: A0 at the left edge, C8 at the right edge.
  3. Keyboard strip1408 × 112 pixels, colour. 1408 / 88 = 16 px per key index.
  4. CNN, 4 blocksEach block halves width and height: 1408×112 → 88×7, with 192 channels. The same CNN runs on every frame.
  5. 88 key columns (the “tiles”)Average each of the 88 columns into one vector of 64 numbers per key per frame — pooling over 88 fixed regions.
  6. Memory over time (BiGRU)One small bidirectional GRU reads each key’s vectors over a 32-frame clip (1.07 s), with the same weights for all 88 keys.
  7. Two outputs per keyOnset probability and frame probability (the frame head also sees the onset). 88 × 2 numbers per frame.
  8. Decode → MIDIA note starts where the onset probability crosses its threshold and lasts while the frame probability stays above its threshold. Both thresholds are chosen on the validation split.

Why we designed it this way

  • Pitch is position. Once the keyboard corners are known, every key sits in a known place, so the model never has to find a key, only to judge it. The 88 regions never move, and nothing is detected.
  • Shared weights. All keys use the same weights, so what the model learns about one key helps the others. This is why 1.3 M parameters are enough, while V2N uses 28.2 M.
  • Geometry is fixed by the keyboard, not by the image. V2N names “geometry-invariant keyboard localization” as its open problem: its warp fixes the pixel grid, while the keyboard inside it varies between datasets. Our warp maps the four keyboard corners to the full strip, so every key sits in the same 16-px column in every video of both datasets.
  • Equal columns are an approximation. Real white keys are wider than black keys, so a 16-px column is not the exact outline of its key. But each output column sees a wider area of the strip (the CNN’s receptive field) and learns which pixels belong to its key. The error analysis confirms this works: only 19 of 42,241 notes are confused with a neighbouring key (Results R10).
  • 30 fps and 1-second context. 30 fps matches PPAN; a 32-frame clip (1.07 s) is close to V2N’s 1-second window. One frame is 33.3 ms, which matters for the 50 ms tolerance.
  • The ablation confirms it. The same network with one global feature instead of the 88 columns (A1) finds fewer notes, especially at the ends of the keyboard, and falls below V2N at 100 ms (Results R11).

How we score (the evaluation protocol)

  1. All choices on the validation split. The model is run once on every validation video; many onset/frame threshold pairs (and, in the timing experiments, time corrections) are tried, and the best is kept. The test set is never used for any choice.
  2. Score whole test videos once with those settings, using mir_eval, the standard library that all three papers use. Our scorer matches notes key by key; it gives exactly the same numbers as mir_eval, 25× faster.
  3. Onset F1 at 50 and 100 ms: a predicted note is correct if its pitch is right and its onset is within ±50 ms (or ±100 ms) of a true note. 50 ms is the audio standard and V2N’s main number; 100 ms is PPAN’s number for PianoYT. At 30 fps one frame is 33.3 ms.
  4. Onset + offset, two rules: mir_eval’s default (offset within 20% of the note length, at least 50 ms) and V2N’s stricter “++Off” (offset within the same ±50 / ±100 ms). We use offsets only on PianoVAM, because PianoYT offsets include the pedal.
  5. Frame F1: every key × time step is one yes/no decision (30 fps grid in our evaluator; V2N’s 60 Hz grid in the V2N comparison).
  6. Mean over recordings, each recording counting once. Two systems are compared with a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test over the recordings, two-sided. V2N used a one-sided test, which is less strict.

R4 · Training curves (validation, per frame)

  • PianoVAM improves quickly and is flat from epoch ~10 (0.80–0.81); more epochs would not help much.
  • PianoYT stops at ~0.35 per-frame onset F1 from epoch 6 on, with a training loss 7× higher than PianoVAM’s (0.128 vs 0.018 at the last epoch). The model cannot fit the labels better — a sign of label noise (timing and wrong notes), not of too little training.
  • Fine-tuning (E4) starts from the PianoVAM model. Its per-frame valid score (best 0.344) is slightly below E2’s 0.35, yet on test it is clearly better (R6). The per-frame score punishes a one-frame timing difference; the note-level valid score agrees with the test (E4 0.370 vs E2 0.357 at 50 ms).

On unfamiliar video the thresholds must be very low (0.20 and 0.05), and there the first crossing is an unreliable onset time while the peak centre is stable; one shift then aligns it.
  • Without timing correction the reverse direction transfers better (E5b 0.146 / 0.210 vs E3b 0.065 / 0.153): a model trained on many different YouTube pianos generalises better to the single PianoVAM setup. With the correction, both directions end up similarly low: 17–19% at 50 ms, 23–27% at 100 ms. What remains is appearance — pianos, lighting, cameras.

What A1 tells us

  • The per-key design is what puts us ahead of V2N. With the same recipe, the global model ties V2N at 50 ms and is significantly below it at 100 ms; the per-key model ties at 50 ms and is significantly above it at 100 ms.
  • The difference is in finding notes, not in timing. Both models make about the same number of timing errors (826 vs 877) and see black keys equally late (~15 ms). The global model misses almost twice as many notes outright and invents 3.7× as many, mostly at the two ends of the keyboard. That is also why its gap is larger at 100 ms than at 50 ms: a wider window cannot recover a note that was never found.
  • A likely reason: in the global model every key has its own output weights, so the rarely played keys at the ends get little training (the lowest octave has 1% of the notes). In the per-key model all 88 keys share one detector, so A0 learns from every note played anywhere on the keyboard.
  • Smaller and better. The per-key model has 2.8× fewer parameters than the global one and 22× fewer than V2N. With R3, it separates two measured contributions for Paper 1: the recipe (84.3 → 95.3 at 50 ms) and the per-key design (95.7 → 98.0 at 100 ms).

What we do differently, and why

  1. Per-key tiles instead of a global transformer. Pitch is position, so each key gets its own column and all keys share weights: a tiny model (1.3 M) that learns from little data and is easy to explain.
  2. Clean pre-training data. They pre-train on R3; we pre-train on PianoVAM, whose labels come from the piano itself. E4 then asks a clear question: does exact-label pre-training help noisy YouTube data?
  3. We also report 50 ms. At 30 fps, 100 ms is ±3 frames and hides timing errors, which our sync audit shows are real in PianoYT (R9).
  4. Thresholds chosen on valid instead of fixed, because the best threshold moves a lot between datasets (0.90 vs 0.20, R5).

What we can say — and what not

We can say

  • PPAN reports 0.68 onset F at 100 ms on PianoYT (R3 pre-training, onset-only fine-tuning).
  • Our E4 — pre-trained on PianoVAM, fine-tuned on PianoYT, 1.3 M parameters — reaches 0.699 at 100 ms on the same 21 test videos: above PPAN’s reported 0.68 and S2S/V2R’s 0.64.
  • Pre-training on PianoVAM significantly improves on PianoYT-only training at 100 ms (0.699 vs 0.651; p = 0.009; 15 of 21 videos).
  • On PianoVAM, PPAN as retrained by V2N scores 84.9 / 94.1 (50 / 100 ms); we score 95.2 / 98.0 (V2N’s numbers for PPAN, not our run).

Say carefully, or not at all

  • “Better than PPAN”: write “above PPAN’s reported score”. No paired test is possible (we have no PPAN predictions), the training data and pre-training differ, and the margin is 0.02.
  • “Significantly better than PianoYT-only training at 50 ms”: with 21 videos it is a trend (p = 0.07), not significant.
  • Zero-shot: their ViT goes R3 → PianoYT at 0.40; ours goes PianoVAM → PianoYT at 0.153 (0.270 with a valid-chosen timing correction). The source datasets differ, so no direct claim is possible.

In one minute

  • A dataset, not a model: 106 solo recordings, about 21 hours, 1,050,966 notes, 10 amateur pianists (70 recordings advanced, 26 intermediate, 10 beginner), practising on a Yamaha Disklavier — a piano that records every key as exact MIDI.

V2N: the current state of the art

Title
Multi-Task Multi-Frame Visual Piano Transcription
Authors
Yonghyun Kim, Hoyeol Sohn, Juhan Nam, Alexander Lerch
Venue
Accepted to ISMIR 2026 (arXiv 4 Aug 2026)
Code
github.com/yonghyunk1m/V2N — includes predicted MIDI, which we used for the head-to-head comparison
Link
arxiv.org/abs/2608.03419

In one minute

  • V2N (“Video to Notes”) calls itself the first complete visual transcription system: onset, offset, key hold and velocity.
  • A ResNet-18 (grayscale, trained from scratch) reads 5-frame windows of an 800 × 144 keyboard image at 25 fps. Three Conformer-style convolution blocks cover 1 second (25 frames), and four BiLSTM heads give the four outputs.
  • 28.2 M parameters, 125 GFLOPs per 1-second clip.
  • They retrained four older systems (S2S, V2R, PPAN, and the video branch of Li et al.) on PianoVAM and R3, and beat them all.
  • They show that cross-dataset transfer collapses (PianoVAM ↔ R3: 0–6%) and blame the geometry: their warp fixes the pixel grid, not the keyboard inside it.

What we do differently, and why

  1. We fix the keyboard, not only the pixel grid. V2N names “geometry-invariant keyboard localization” as future work. Our warp maps the keyboard corners to the full strip, so every key sits in the same 16-px column in every video, and our crop check confirms it on both datasets (median error 0.36 and 0.21 white keys).
  2. …and show that geometry is not enough. Our zero-shot still drops (15.3% at 100 ms). One timing correction chosen on the target’s validation split recovers a large part (27.0%, E3c); the rest is appearance (pianos, light, cameras). V2N does not separate these causes; we can.
  3. Per-key tiles instead of a global feature. 1.3 M parameters instead of 28.2 M, with equal or better onset and offset scores on the same test. Our ablation A1 shows that this design is what makes the difference: with one global feature, the same network falls below V2N at 100 ms (95.7 vs 97.0, p = 0.004).
  4. PianoYT. V2N does not use it. Clean vs noisy labels, fine-tuning and label sync are our territory.
  5. Sync as a topic. V2N found MIDI–video offsets above 200 ms in 70 of 895 R3 files and lists “joint sync alignment for noisy training data” as future work. Our sync audit (31 of 220 PianoYT videos) and E6 / E6ft address it; correcting the training labels did not help our fine-tuned model.

What we can say — and what not

We can say

  • On the same 9 test recordings, labels and scoring script, our 1.3 M model matches V2N at 50 ms (95.2 vs 94.7, p = 0.91) and beats it at 100 ms (98.0 vs 97.0, p = 0.008) and on onset + offset at 100 ms (97.1 vs 95.5, p = 0.004).
  • Our 98.0 at 100 ms is above every number in V2N’s table (the best is Li et al.’s video branch, 97.4), but we tested significance only against V2N.
  • With one global feature instead of the 88 key columns (A1), the same network is significantly below V2N at 100 ms (95.7 vs 97.0, p = 0.004): the per-key design, not the recipe alone, is what beats V2N.

We cannot say

  • “Better than V2N at 50 ms” — it is a tie.
  • That tuning the timing closes the 50 ms gap — we tried (E1t), and it did not transfer from valid to test.
  • “Better overall” — V2N also predicts velocity (82.8 / 84.5) and was tested on R3; we did neither.
  • Anything strong from n = 9 alone: say “on the 9 test recordings”, and add PianoYT and the ablation as further evidence.

Glossary

TermMeaning in this report
Scores
Onset F1 (note F1)A predicted note is correct if its pitch is right and its start is within the tolerance of a true note (each true note matched once). F1 = harmonic mean of precision and recall. The main number of all three papers.
@50 ms / @100 msThe onset tolerance: ±50 ms (audio standard, V2N’s main number) or ±100 ms (PPAN’s PianoYT number). At 30 fps one frame is 33.3 ms.
Precision (P), recall (R)P = share of predicted notes that are correct; R = share of true notes that were found.
Onset + offset, “++Off”Also the end of the note must be right: within 20% of its length (at least 50 ms, the mir_eval default), or within the same ±50 / ±100 ms (V2N’s “++Off”).
Frame F1Every key × time step is one yes/no decision (“is the key down?”).
Per-frame validation F1The score logged after each training epoch: counted per key and frame on 32-frame clips. A note one frame late is wrong here but right in the note score, so it is lower than note F1 and only compares epochs of one run.
Paired Wilcoxon testCompares two systems recording by recording (9 on PianoVAM, 21 on PianoYT) without assuming normal distributions. p < 0.05 = the difference is unlikely to be chance. We use the two-sided version.
Training and evaluation
Train / valid / testTraining data; the validation split, used for every choice (checkpoint, thresholds, timing); the test split, scored once at the end.
Thresholds (calibration)The probability above which the model’s onset (and frame) output counts as a note, chosen on the validation split.
Timing correctionA time shift (and optionally a sub-frame onset rule and an extra shift for black keys) chosen on the validation split and added to every predicted note.
Zero-shotTesting on a dataset the model was never trained on.
Fine-tuningContinuing to train an already trained model on a new dataset, with a smaller learning rate.
BatchNorm statisticsRunning averages of the network’s internal activations, updated during training and used instead of per-batch values when the model is evaluated. One overflowing training step can corrupt them, which is what happened to E6.
RecipeEverything about training except the architecture: data per video, resolution, augmentation, learning-rate schedule, epochs.
Per-key (tiled) vs global (strip)Per-key: the image features are pooled into 88 fixed columns, one per key, sharing weights. Global: one description of the whole keyboard per frame predicts all 88 keys (V2N, PPAN, our ablation A1).
Sync / lagThe time offset between a video’s picture and its labels. PianoYT labels come from the audio, so an audio–video offset in the upload becomes a label offset.
Datasets and systems
PianoVAM106 recordings of amateur pianists on a Disklavier (ISMIR 2025); labels = the piano’s own MIDI. We use the v1.1 split (80 / 9 / 9).
PianoYT228 YouTube piano videos (Audeo, NeurIPS 2020); labels = audio transcription of each video’s sound. Official split 207 train / 21 test.
R3About 31 h of video of two professional pianists with real MIDI; used by PPAN and V2N, not by us.
S2S, V2R, Li et al.Earlier visual transcription systems, retrained and reported by V2N and PPAN.
PPAN“Pay Attention to the Keys” (IJCAI 2025), a video transformer; the best published PianoYT result (0.68 at 100 ms).
V2N“Multi-Task Multi-Frame Visual Piano Transcription” (ISMIR 2026), the state of the art on PianoVAM.
